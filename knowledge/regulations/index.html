National Bank of Ethiopia directives

Directives issued by the National Bank of Ethiopia (NBE) set the day-to-day rules for foreign exchange, interest rates, bank licensing, foreign participation in banking, payments and microfinance. Since the July 2024 move to a market-based exchange rate, these directives have changed often and directly affect how investors bring in capital, pay for imports, borrow abroad and repatriate profits.
  • Foreign exchangeAmended by FXD/3/2025, FXD/04/2026, FXD/05/2026 and FVD/01/2026

    Foreign Exchange Directive

    Directive No. FXD/01/2024 · National Bank of Ethiopia · July 29, 2024

    The core rulebook of Ethiopia's market-based foreign exchange regime, effective July 29, 2024. It lets banks buy and sell foreign currency at freely negotiated rates, with the NBE publishing only an indicative daily rate. Exporters must repatriate proceeds within three months, convert 50% to Birr and may keep 50% in a retention account; the directive also covers FX accounts for non-resident Ethiopians and foreign nationals of Ethiopian origin, independent forex bureaus, external loans (60:40 debt-to-equity cap for foreign investors) and dividend repatriation. It repealed all earlier NBE foreign exchange directives and circulars.

    Why it matters: This is the starting point for any question on bringing in capital, opening FX accounts, paying for imports, borrowing abroad or sending dividends home. Read it together with its later amendments, which have relaxed many approval requirements.

  • Foreign exchangeIn force

    Amendment to Foreign Exchange Directive No. FXD/01/2024

    Directive No. FXD/05/2026 · National Bank of Ethiopia · May 25, 2026

    Effective May 25, 2026, banks may approve letters of credit on acceptance and cash against documents on acceptance without prior NBE approval for institutions with foreign currency accounts, including retention account holders. These institutions may also order or initiate shipments before bank approval, with payment processed after documents are submitted and verified. An accompanying NBE notice says banks must price LC fees on an annualized basis, pro-rated to each LC's tenor.

    Why it matters: Importers that hold their own FX or retention balances face fewer approval steps and can move goods faster. Trade finance becomes a bank-level decision rather than a central-bank one.

  • Foreign exchangeIn force

    Import on Franco Valuta Directive

    Directive No. FVD/01/2026 · National Bank of Ethiopia · May 29, 2026

    Effective May 29, 2026, this directive sets the rules for franco valuta imports, meaning goods imported without drawing foreign exchange from the domestic banking system. Eligible users include licensed domestic, diaspora and foreign investors, foreign-owned manufacturers, FDI and diaspora traders, strategic projects, diplomatic missions and NGOs, for capital goods, raw materials, trade goods and other listed items. Users need valid licenses and must file documents with Customs; transactions must be recorded digitally, and misuse can lead to fines, confiscation or criminal liability. It repeals sub-article 8.1.3 of FXD/01/2024.

    Why it matters: Investors and traders with their own offshore foreign currency can import machinery, inputs and, for FDI and diaspora traders, trade goods without competing for bank FX. Annex limits apply to personal effects, samples and gifts.

  • Foreign exchangeIn force

    Amendment to Foreign Exchange Directive No. FXD/01/2024

    Directive No. FXD/04/2026 · National Bank of Ethiopia · February 11, 2026

    Effective February 12, 2026, this broad relaxation lets service exporters keep 100% of proceeds in retention accounts indefinitely, allows banks to offer forward rates, and lets FDI companies open FX accounts with only an application, investment license and TIN. Banks may now approve dividend and net profit remittances, external loans and supplier credits without NBE approval (with monthly reporting), and may issue private FX loan guarantees up to 10% of total capital. It removes the minimum FX account deposit and the USD 10,000 cash declaration requirement, permits outward investment case by case with NBE approval, and allows residents to send up to USD 3,000 abroad for family support.

    Why it matters: Profit repatriation and offshore borrowing are now handled at the commercial bank, which should shorten timelines for foreign investors. Service exporters keep full control of their FX earnings.

    Link to the original document: available to members

  • Interest ratesIn force

    Interest Rates (As Amended) Directive

    Directive No. NBE/INT/13/2026 · National Bank of Ethiopia · January 9, 2026

    Effective January 9, 2026, each bank freely sets its demand, savings and time deposit rates and its lending rates, so there is no longer a minimum savings rate. Rates must be approved in writing by each bank's board, based on explicit written criteria, and reported to the NBE, along with monthly weighted average rates. Rates on NBE lending and standing facilities are set by the NBE, and interbank rates are negotiated between banks. It repeals and replaces all earlier NBE interest rate directives.

    Why it matters: Borrowing costs and deposit returns are now market-determined and can differ across banks, so it pays to compare offers. Existing loans keep their contractual terms unless the contract provides otherwise.

    Link to the original document: available to members

  • Banking licensingIn force

    Requirements for Licensing and Renewal of Banking Business and Representative Office Directive

    Directive No. SBB/94/2025 · National Bank of Ethiopia · June 25, 2025

    Effective June 25, 2025, this directive sets the licensing path for domestic banks, foreign bank subsidiaries, foreign bank branches and representative offices. Minimum paid-up capital is Birr 5 billion for each type of bank, paid in acceptable foreign currency for foreign subsidiaries and branches; representative offices need at least USD 100,000 remitted in. Foreign nationals and foreign-owned Ethiopian organizations may together hold up to 49% of a domestic bank, with direct caps of 7% for an individual, 10% for a company and 40% for a strategic investor. It also sets licensing and renewal fees and repeals Directive No. SBB/56/2013.

    Why it matters: This is the main entry rulebook for foreign banks and for foreign investors buying into Ethiopian banks. It defines the capital, ownership limits and fees to plan for.

    Link to the original document: available to members

  • Foreign equity in banksDraft

    Equity Investment by Foreign Nationals and Foreign Owned Ethiopian Organizations Directive (Zero Draft)

    Directive No. SBB/XXX/2025 (draft) · National Bank of Ethiopia · 2025-10

    This draft, issued under Banking Business Proclamation No. 1360/2025, would set detailed rules for foreign share purchases in Ethiopian banks. It proposes direct caps of 7% for a foreign individual, 10% for a foreign company and 40% for a strategic investor, with an aggregate foreign cap of 49% outside foreign bank subsidiaries. Shares would have to be paid in US dollars, euros or pounds sterling through the banking system, and holdings of 2% or more would need NBE approval. Foreign nationals of Ethiopian origin investing in Birr as domestic investors would not be subject to these limits.

    Why it matters: Signals how the NBE intends to regulate portfolio and strategic stakes in local banks. Treat it as a draft; check for a final version before investing.

  • Banking licensingIn force

    Requirements for Licensing a Bank Branch to Operate in Special Economic Zone Directive

    Directive No. SBB/99/2026 · National Bank of Ethiopia · January 6, 2026

    Effective January 6, 2026, any bank, including foreign bank subsidiaries and branches, needs a branch license before operating in a Special Economic Zone. Applicants must meet prudential tests such as at least 1% of banking sector assets (waived for new foreign banks in their first two years), a capital adequacy ratio two points above the minimum, and a sound record on settling FX obligations. Zone branches must offer foreign exchange services and serve only zone residents; existing zone branches have two years to comply.

    Why it matters: Companies operating in SEZs should see full banking and FX services on site. Banks targeting zone clients must meet these criteria first.

    Link to the original document: available to members

  • Capital adequacyIn force

    Risk Based Capital Adequacy Requirement for Banks Directive

    Directive No. SBB/95/2025 · National Bank of Ethiopia · November 7, 2025

    Issued under Banking Business Proclamation No. 1360/2025, this directive moves banks to a risk-based capital framework covering credit, market and operational risk. Banks must hold Common Equity Tier 1 of at least 7%, Tier 1 of at least 9% and total capital of at least 11% of risk-weighted assets. Credit risk uses a standardized approach, for example 0% for Birr sovereign exposures, 35% for qualifying residential mortgages and 100% for unrated corporates.

    Why it matters: Shapes how much capital banks need, and so how much they can lend and how they price loans. Investors in bank shares should expect capital raises at banks near these minimums.

    Link to the original document: available to members

  • Foreign exchangeIn force

    Foreign Exchange Exposure Limits of Banks Directive

    Directive No. SBB/96/2025 · National Bank of Ethiopia · November 7, 2025

    Effective November 10, 2025, a bank's overall open foreign currency position may not exceed plus or minus 18% of its Tier 1 capital at the close of each business day. Banks set their own single-currency limits within that cap, must square any excess by the next business day and report daily. Breaches and late or false reporting carry fines. It repeals Directive No. SBB/27/2001.

    Why it matters: Limits how far banks can run long or short FX positions, which affects their appetite for FX trades and forward contracts with corporate clients.

    Link to the original document: available to members

  • Banking regulationIn force

    Investment on DBE Bonds (Repealing) Directive

    Directive No. SBB/98/2025 · National Bank of Ethiopia · December 31, 2025

    Effective December 31, 2025, this directive repeals Directive No. SBB/81/2021, which required banks to invest in Development Bank of Ethiopia bonds. Banks that fell short of the benchmark (1% of audited outstanding loans and advances, based on June 30, 2025 positions) had to settle the shortfall by January 31, 2026. Bonds bought before the effective date stay under the 2021 rules.

    Why it matters: Ends a mandatory allocation of bank funds, which frees liquidity for private-sector lending over time.

    Link to the original document: available to members

  • Corporate governanceIn force

    Bank Corporate Governance Directive

    Directive No. SBB/91/2024 · National Bank of Ethiopia · June 12, 2024

    Effective June 12, 2024, banks must have at least nine directors: one-third elected by non-influential shareholders, one-third by all shareholders and one-third independent, with at least two women. A shareholder with 2% or more is an influential shareholder, terms last up to three years and no director may serve more than six consecutive years. Proxy voting is capped at 10% of subscribed capital. It repeals Directive No. SBB/71/2019.

    Why it matters: Anyone taking a significant stake in an Ethiopian bank should understand how board seats are allocated and what NBE approvals directors need.

    Link to the original document: available to members

  • Payments and mobile moneyIn force; payment instrument issuer rules further amended by Directive No. ONPS/10/2025

    Payment Instrument Issuer (Amendment) Directive

    Directive No. ONPS/09/2023 · National Bank of Ethiopia · 2023-10

    According to the NBE's October 2023 announcement of this revised directive for mobile money providers, it adds requirements for foreign mobile money providers in line with National Payment System (Amendment) Proclamation No. 1282/2023. It raised the daily wallet balance limit from Birr 30,000 to Birr 75,000, set a daily aggregate transaction limit of Birr 150,000 (with exemptions such as utility, tax and bulk payments), and allows banks to set up dedicated mobile money subsidiaries. It also lets providers offer additional services such as capital market products.

    Why it matters: Opens the mobile money market to foreign providers and bank subsidiaries, and sets the wallet limits that shape consumer payment products.

    Link to the original document: available to members

  • MicrofinanceIn force

    Minimum Paid-up Capital and Capital Adequacy Requirements of Microfinance Institutions Directive (3rd Replacement)

    Directive No. MFI/36/2023 · National Bank of Ethiopia · January 16, 2023

    Effective January 16, 2023, a new microfinance license requires at least Birr 75 million of paid-up capital, fully paid in cash. Existing microfinance institutions below that level must reach it by January 2028, and all institutions must keep a capital adequacy ratio of at least 12% and report quarterly. Institutions that fail the requirement may be put under receivership or merged. It repeals Directive No. MFI/27/2015.

    Why it matters: Sets the entry ticket for investors planning a microfinance institution and points to consolidation among smaller existing ones.

Ministry of Finance: proclamations, regulations and directives

Federal tax, budget, public-private partnership and investment-incentive laws and directives that Ethiopia's Ministry of Finance issues or administers. Proclamations are enacted by Parliament (the House of Peoples' Representatives) and administered by the Ministry; directives are issued by the Ministry itself. Together they set how much tax investors pay, how incentives are granted, and how PPP projects are procured.
  • BudgetEnacted for budget year 2026/27; proclamation number left blank in the posted copy

    2019 Fiscal Year Federal Government Budget Proclamation (Part One)

    House of Peoples' Representatives (enacted by Parliament); posted and administered by the Ministry of Finance · 2026-07

    This is the federal budget law for Ethiopian fiscal year 2019, which runs from 1 Hamle 2018 to 30 Sene 2019 E.C. (July 2026 to July 2027). It authorizes total spending of Birr 2,339.3 billion: Birr 1,236.5 billion recurrent, Birr 568.3 billion capital, Birr 520.6 billion in subsidies to regions, and Birr 14 billion for the Sustainable Development Goals. Like all proclamations, it is enacted by Parliament; the Ministry of Finance prepares and administers it. A Part Two spreadsheet with line-item detail is posted alongside it.

    Why it matters: The budget shows where federal capital spending and procurement opportunities will be in 2026/27, and how much the government needs to borrow and collect in taxes.

    Link to the original document: available to members

  • Tax administrationDraft (second draft posted May 15, 2026); enactment not confirmed

    Federal Tax Administration (Amendment) Proclamation (draft, Amharic)

    Draft amendment to Proclamation No. 983/2016 · Ministry of Finance (draft for enactment by the House of Peoples' Representatives) · 2026-05

    This draft would amend the Federal Tax Administration Proclamation No. 983/2016, which Parliament enacted and the Ministry administers. It would add a mediation process for tax disputes (generally to be completed within 60 days) and allow reassessment within 10 years where fraud is proven and 5 years otherwise. It would also limit new evidence at the appeal stage, allow conditional tax clearance certificates, and support electronic and QR-coded receipts. Penalties would rise, including a 100,000 birr fine per receipt not issued, and company managers and finance heads would be presumed liable for tax offences.

    Why it matters: If enacted, the draft changes audit exposure, how disputes are settled, and the personal liability of directors and finance staff. It also requires banks to see a tax clearance certificate before foreign investors repatriate profits or sale proceeds.

  • TaxIn force

    Income Tax (Amendment) Proclamation

    Proclamation No. 1395/2025 · House of Peoples' Representatives (enacted by Parliament); administered by the Ministry of Finance · 2025

    This proclamation amends the Federal Income Tax Proclamation No. 979/2016. It is enacted by Parliament and administered by the Ministry of Finance. It sets the Category A taxpayer threshold at annual turnover above Birr 2,000,000 and treats services and construction activities as a permanent establishment once they exceed 91 days in a tax year. It treats income from digital services that non-residents provide in Ethiopia as Ethiopian-source income, and it replaces the employment and rental income tax rate tables (top rate 35%).

    Why it matters: Foreign service providers, digital businesses and contractors working in Ethiopia face wider tax exposure, and employers must apply the new payroll brackets. The posted scan is hard to read, so check exact rates against the gazetted text.

    Link to the original document: available to members

  • TaxIn force

    Value Added Tax Proclamation

    Proclamation No. 1341/2024 · House of Peoples' Representatives (enacted by Parliament); administered by the Ministry of Finance · August 21, 2024

    This new VAT law, published in the Federal Negarit Gazette (30th Year No. 61) on 21 August 2024, replaces the earlier VAT framework. Parliament enacted it and the Ministry of Finance administers it. It keeps the 15% standard rate and a 0% rate for zero-rated supplies (Schedule 1), and lists exempt supplies and imports in Schedules 2 and 3. Registration is compulsory once taxable supplies exceed Birr 2,000,000 in 12 months, and the law covers remote and electronic supplies, including reverse charge on some services from overseas providers.

    Why it matters: Most businesses must register, charge VAT and file returns under this law. Foreign digital and e-commerce suppliers selling into Ethiopia now fall within its scope.

    Link to the original document: available to members

  • TaxIn force

    Directive on Registration for Value Added Tax

    Directive No. 1104/2025 (1104/2017 E.C.) · Ministry of Finance · 2025-09

    Issued under Article 12(2) of VAT Proclamation No. 1341/2024, this directive says who must register for VAT. That includes Category A income taxpayers, taxpayers required to keep books of account or who keep them voluntarily, and anyone whose combined taxable and exempt turnover exceeds Birr 2,000,000. Existing taxpayers covered by the directive had 30 days from its effective date to register.

    Why it matters: Companies that keep books must register for VAT whatever their turnover, which brings many small and mid-sized firms and their suppliers into the VAT system.

    Link to the original document: available to members

  • TaxIn force

    A Directive to Determine the Amount of Electricity and Water Consumption for Domestic Use Exempt from Value Added Tax

    Directive No. 1021/2024 · Ministry of Finance · September 5, 2024

    Issued under Annex 2 of VAT Proclamation No. 1341/2024, this directive exempts monthly household consumption of up to 200 kWh of electricity and 15 cubic meters of water (excluding bottled water) from VAT. Electricity generators and transmitters must collect and remit VAT on wholesale supplies.

    Why it matters: Commercial and industrial electricity and water use is subject to VAT. Utilities and power producers must collect VAT on wholesale supplies.

  • Transfer pricingIn force

    A Directive to Provide Rules on Transfer Pricing

    Directive No. 981/2024 · Ministry of Finance · 2024-01

    Issued under Article 79 of the Federal Income Tax Proclamation No. 979/2016, this directive replaces Transfer Pricing Directive No. 231/2021. It applies the arm's length principle to domestic and cross-border transactions between related persons with annual turnover above Birr 500,000. It sets out five approved pricing methods, rules for intra-group services and intangibles, and advance pricing arrangements valid for up to five tax periods. Contemporaneous documentation must be ready by the return filing date and provided within 45 days of a request.

    Why it matters: Multinational subsidiaries and related-party groups in Ethiopia must price intra-group dealings at arm's length and keep transfer pricing files. Adjustments can only increase taxable income, and penalties apply for missing documentation.

    Link to the original document: available to members

  • ExciseIn force

    The Excise Stamp Management Directive

    Directive No. 1004/2024 · Ministry of Finance · 2024-05

    Issued under the Excise Tax Proclamation No. 1186/2020 (enacted by Parliament and administered by the Ministry), this directive requires digital excise stamps with unique identifiers and a track-and-trace system. It covers spirits, beer, wine, ready-to-drink alcoholic beverages, tobacco products, bottled water and carbonated or sweetened soft drinks. Manufacturers and importers apply to the Tax Authority, pay stamp fees, fund on-site system equipment, and must stamp goods at the factory or at customs. Goods without the required stamp may be seized after a 120-day transition.

    Why it matters: Beverage, tobacco and bottled-water producers and importers face new compliance costs, production-line controls and seizure risk.

    Link to the original document: available to members

  • ExciseIn force

    A Directive Issued to Make Adjustments to the Specific Rates of Excise Tax

    Directive No. 1007/2024 · Ministry of Finance · 2024-06

    Under Article 10(2) of the Excise Tax Proclamation No. 1186/2020, which requires at least yearly inflation indexing, this directive raises the specific excise rates on alcohol, tobacco and plastic bags using the GDP deflator. Examples: malt beer pays 40% or Birr 28 per liter, whichever is higher; cigarettes pay 30% plus Birr 20 per pack of 20; plastic shopping bags pay Birr 103 per kg. Lower beer rates apply where local barley or at least 75% local raw material is used.

    Why it matters: Brewers and tobacco firms should expect yearly increases in specific rates. The lower rates for local inputs reward sourcing raw materials locally.

    Link to the original document: available to members

  • ExciseIn force

    Excise Tax Management System Directive

    Directive No. 1071/2025 (1071/2017 E.C.) · Ministry of Finance · 2025-05

    Issued under the Excise Tax Proclamation, this directive requires dealers in excisable goods to hold a license for listed activities. It sets security, storage, stock-record and waste-disposal rules for licensed premises. The Tax Authority may require seals, cameras, flow meters and remote-view access, and may require automated production in some industries. Existing importers and manufacturers are deemed licensed.

    Why it matters: Excisable-goods manufacturers and importers must meet licensing and monitoring requirements. The Amharic and English texts differ on some figures, so check them against the official text.

    Link to the original document: available to members

  • IncentivesIn force

    Directive for the Implementation of Investment Incentives

    Directive No. 1064/2025 (1064/2017 E.C.) · Ministry of Finance · May 26, 2025

    Issued under Investment Incentives Regulation No. 517/2022, this directive sets the procedure for income tax holidays and customs duty exemptions. It replaces earlier agency and Investment Board decisions on the subject. It lists the documents investors must provide, the grounds and procedure for revoking incentives (with a 30-day appeal), and the duty-free construction materials allowed, which are imported in 30/30/40 stages. Investors must buy local capital goods when they are available.

    Why it matters: This is the working rulebook for claiming and keeping tax holidays and duty-free imports. Investors who lose an incentive must repay the exempted taxes with interest and penalties.

    Link to the original document: available to members

  • IncentivesDraft

    Council of Ministers Regulation on Investment Incentives (revised draft)

    Draft regulation (number blank) · Council of Ministers (draft posted by the Ministry of Finance) · 2025-10

    This draft would replace tax holidays with an outcome-based system. The tools would be reduced income tax rates, a capital cost allowance, and exemptions from minimum tax, dividend tax and capital gains tax, as well as customs duty relief. It proposes, for example, a 5% rate for Special Economic Zone developers and startups for 10 years, and reduced rates for listed manufacturing sectors, exporters and renewable-energy users. Incentives would be tied to signed performance agreements that the government could revoke if targets are not met.

    Why it matters: If adopted, the draft would reshape the incentive package for new investments. Investors planning projects should follow it closely.

    Link to the original document: available to members

  • PPPIn force

    Directive on Structural Cost Reduction and Enhanced Transparency in the Power Sector

    Directive No. 1067/2025 (1067/2017 E.C.) · Ministry of Finance · May 26, 2025

    Issued under Article 65(2) of the PPP Proclamation No. 1076/2018, this directive requires new power investments to follow an integrated least-cost plan approved by the PPP Board. It requires competitive procurement of new PPP power projects, with limited published exemptions. The Ethiopian Energy and Petroleum Authority approves power purchase agreements and reviews tariffs, and state utilities must compete openly with private players.

    Why it matters: Independent power producers get a more transparent, competitive route into the sector. Projects outside the plan need special validation and Board approval.

    Link to the original document: available to members

  • PPPIn force (repeal provisions not legible in the posted copy)

    Directive Issued to Implement the Public-Private Partnership Proclamation

    Directive No. 242/2013 (E.C.) · Ministry of Finance · 2021

    Issued under Article 65(2) of the PPP Proclamation in 2013 E.C. (2020/21), this Amharic directive sets out how the nine-member PPP Board and the PPP Directorate General operate. It covers the approval stages from identification to tender, prequalification and bid evaluation, with a local-content preference of up to 7%. It also covers agreement terms (typically 20 to 30 years), direct negotiation under strict conditions, unsolicited proposals and ethics rules.

    Why it matters: Private bidders on Ethiopian PPPs should read it for the procurement steps, the evaluation rules and the treatment of unsolicited proposals.

  • PPPIn force, as amended (amendment Proclamation No. 1283/2023 is posted by the Ministry but its scan could not be read)

    Public Private Partnership Proclamation

    Proclamation No. 1076/2018 · House of Peoples' Representatives (enacted by Parliament); administered by the Ministry of Finance · February 22, 2018

    Published in the Federal Negarit Gazette (24th Year No. 28) on 22 February 2018, this proclamation sets up Ethiopia's PPP framework. Parliament enacted it and the Ministry of Finance administers it. It creates a PPP Board chaired by the Finance Minister and a PPP Directorate General within the Ministry. Contracting authorities run feasibility and value-for-money studies, and projects are procured by open, competitive bidding after Board approval. It excludes oil, mining, air space rights and privatization.

    Why it matters: This is the core law for private investors bidding on infrastructure and public-service concessions in Ethiopia, including how government support and incentives are requested.

    Link to the original document: available to members

  • Tax policyPublished (policy)

    National Medium-Term Revenue Strategy, FY 2024/25 to FY 2027/28

    Ministry of Finance and Ministry of Revenue · 2024-08

    The strategy aims to reverse Ethiopia's falling tax-to-GDP ratio, targeting about 13.2% by FY 2027/28 through tax policy measures (about 3% of GDP) and administration measures (about 2.9% of GDP). Planned steps include property and vehicle taxes, fewer VAT and import exemptions, a minimum alternative tax, a move to global income taxation, inflation-indexed excise rates and fewer tariff bands. It also plans e-invoicing and integrated tax and customs systems.

    Why it matters: It signals the direction of upcoming tax laws, including fewer exemptions and wider bases, which investors should factor into long-term plans.

    Link to the original document: available to members

  • Public debtPublished (statistics)

    Public Sector Debt Statistical Bulletin No. 56

    Bulletin No. 56 · Ministry of Finance (Debt Management Division) · 2026-02

    The bulletin reports total public sector debt of USD 52.1 billion at 31 December 2025, down 2.0% from June 2025. External debt was USD 34.5 billion (66%) and domestic debt USD 17.7 billion (34%). Investment proclamations and regulations

The core federal laws and Ethiopian Investment Board directives that decide who may invest in what, on what terms, and through which institutions. They cover the sectors reserved for domestic investors, the 2024-2025 openings of import, export, wholesale and retail trade to foreign investors, special economic zones, capital markets, banking and PPPs.
  • Foreign investment openingsIn force (repeals Directive No. 1001/2024)

    Ethiopian Investment Board Directive to Regulate Foreign Investors' Participation in Restricted Export, Import, Wholesale and Retail Trade Investments

    Directive No. 1082/2025 · Ethiopian Investment Board · 2025

    Opens to foreign investors the export, import, wholesale and retail activities that the Investment Regulation reserves for domestic investors. Fertilizer (import and wholesale) and petroleum (import) remain closed. Exporting raw coffee, oilseeds, khat and pulses, and all import and wholesale trade, require a due diligence report on the investor's integrity and capacity. Retail requires at least USD 2.5 million in paid-up capital and a due diligence report from a verification agency the Commission recommends; the Board may approve smaller reputable single-brand retailers case by case.

    Why it matters: This is the current rulebook for foreign trading, distribution and retail businesses. It replaces the 2024 volume and floor-space tests with a capital threshold and due diligence screening.

    Link to the original document: available to members

  • Special economic zonesIn force

    Special Economic Zone Designation, Land Allocation and Utilization Directive

    Directive No. 1083/2025 · Ethiopian Investment Board · 2025

    Sets the criteria and procedure for designating special economic zones under Proclamation No. 1322/2024. A developer must commit at least USD 75 million to zone infrastructure (sub-developers USD 30 million), place a refundable deposit of USD 500,000 (USD 200,000 for sub-developers), and hold at least 50 hectares, with waivers possible for priority sectors such as digital tech and ICT. The Board decides within three months. Zones must export at least 50% of annual gross output, and developers must start construction within six months and finish main works within 36 months. Existing industrial parks are deemed manufacturing SEZs.

    Why it matters: Zone developers and sub-developers face specific capital, land, export and local-content conditions. Enterprises buying or leasing zone land need Commission approval.

  • Special economic zonesIn force

    Special Economic Zone Proclamation

    Proclamation No. 1322/2024 · House of Peoples' Representatives (Federal Negarit Gazette, 30th Year No. 25) · May 20, 2024

    Creates the legal framework for special economic zones: industrial parks, free trade and logistics zones, science and technology parks, service parks, and agriculture and livestock zones. In free trade zones, goods and services can be imported, processed and re-exported free of customs duties and taxes. The Ethiopian Investment Board designates zones on application by a private, public or PPP company that commits at least USD 75 million. The Commission checks applications within 5 days and reviews them within 30, and the Board decides within 30 days. Existing industrial parks keep their zone status without reapplying.

    Why it matters: This is the main law for investors developing, operating or locating in zones. Rules on zone incentives and land are set out in the directives and regulations that implement it.

  • Investment frameworkIn force

    Investment Proclamation

    Proclamation No. 1180/2020 · House of Peoples' Representatives (Federal Negarit Gazette, 26th Year No. 28) · April 2, 2020

    Ethiopia's main investment law. All sectors are open to foreign investors unless a regulation reserves them (Art. 6), and the Investment Board may revise that list. Foreign investors need at least USD 200,000 for a project alone or USD 150,000 jointly with a domestic investor, or USD 100,000 alone (USD 50,000 jointly) for architecture, engineering, technical consultancy, testing or publishing (Art. 9). The law guarantees compensation at market value for expropriation (Art. 19), remittance of profits and capital (Art. 20) and ownership of immovable property other than land (Art. 18).

    Why it matters: It sets entry thresholds, permit requirements and protections for every foreign investor, and it is the legal basis for the Investment Board directives that open restricted sectors.

Investment incentives

Tax and customs incentives for investors in Ethiopia: income tax relief, duty-free capital goods, loss carry-forward, and special treatment for zones and exporters.     Credit No. IDA-78750 / Grant No. IDA-E4850 · World Bank · 2026

    This World Bank document confirms that Ethiopia met the reform conditions for the second tranche. One condition was investment incentive reform. The revised Income Tax Proclamation repeals the tax holidays and incentives granted under earlier instruments, and a new Investment Incentive Regulation replaces all discretionary incentives with a performance-based regime.     Ministry of Finance (draft for the Council of Ministers) · 2025-10

    This is the Ministry of Finance consultation draft of the new performance-based regime. It proposes a 5% income tax rate for SEZ developers (10 years in Addis Ababa and Sheger City, 15 years elsewhere) and for startups. SEZ enterprises would pay 15% in Addis Ababa and Sheger City and 10% elsewhere. Customs duty and tax exemptions would continue for capital goods, construction materials and vehicles, and investors would have to sign performance agreements with the investment authority.

    Directive for the Implementation of Investment Incentives

    Directive No. 1064/2025 (1064/2017 E.C.) · Ministry of Finance · 2025-05

    Sets procedures for income tax holidays and customs duty exemptions under Investment Incentives Regulation No. 517/2022. Tax holidays require an investment license, a business license and a completion letter from the investment authority, and they can be revoked with repayment for fraud or failure to file. Losses incurred during a holiday can be carried forward over half the incentive period. Annex 1 sets SEZ holiday lengths by location (for example, 10 or 15 years for developers). Duty-free construction materials are released in stages, duty-free capital goods are limited to an annexed list, and investors must buy domestic equivalents where they are available.

    Why it matters: This is the practical rulebook for claiming and keeping tax and customs exemptions. Investors licensed under the 2022 regime should keep to its documentation and export-share tests: 80% exports for industrial park enterprises and 60% for others.

    Ethiopia Macro Poverty Outlook, April 2026

    World Bank · 2026-04

    The World Bank estimates GDP growth at 9.2 percent in FY25 and forecasts 8.0 percent for FY26. Inflation fell to just under 10 percent in February 2026, and reserves reached US$6.4 billion (2.8 months of imports) at end-December 2025.     World Bank (IDA) and International Monetary Fund staffs · 2025-09
